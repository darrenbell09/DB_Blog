Everyone is buying AI tools. Very few organizations are getting value from them.

The pattern in small and mid-sized businesses is consistent. Leadership sees AI as a competitive necessity, approves a purchase, and hands IT a new set of licenses to deploy. Within a few months, adoption is low, nobody can articulate what problem the tool is solving, and IT is fielding questions about why the investment is not paying off.

The technology was never the problem. The sequence was.

---

## What Microsoft's Copilot Adoption Methodology Actually Says

Microsoft publishes a Copilot adoption methodology specifically to address this. Most organizations that buy Microsoft Copilot have never read it.

The methodology is built around three phases: Envision, Onboard, and Drive Value. The sequence is intentional.

**Envision** is where the real groundwork happens, and it comes before a deployment decision is made. This phase covers executive sponsorship, defined use cases tied to business outcomes, and measurable success criteria. It also covers the technical prerequisites: permissions audited across SharePoint and OneDrive, sensitivity labels and data loss prevention policies configured, Microsoft Entra ID properly set up, MFA enforced for every user, and Microsoft 365 Apps current on all devices. The licenses are purchased here too, once the business case is established and the environment is ready to receive them.

**Onboard** is where deployment begins. Champions are identified and trained before broader rollout. Users receive training before they are handed a new tool. The licenses purchased in Envision are assigned to the pilot group. The groundwork from Envision is what makes this phase work. Without it, Onboard becomes a rollout into an unprepared environment.

**Drive Value** is where you measure against the success criteria from Envision, expand to additional users, and iterate based on what you learn.

The deployment happens in Onboard. Everything that makes deployment successful happens in Envision. Most SMBs skip Envision entirely.

---

## What Most SMBs Actually Do

They buy the licenses first and figure out the rest later.

No executive AI strategy. No defined use cases. No permissions audit. No data loss prevention policies. No training. No governance framework. Just a purchase order and an expectation that IT will figure out the rest.

The result is predictable. Employees either do not use the tool because nobody explained why they should, or they use it in ways that create risk because nobody established guardrails.

A few months in, leadership asks IT why adoption is low. IT has no good answer because they were never given the organizational conditions that make adoption possible.

---

## The Risk Nobody Is Talking About

The gap between "we bought AI" and "we prepared for AI" is not just a wasted investment problem. It is a security and compliance problem.

When there is no AI policy and no governance framework in place, employees make their own decisions about what tools to use and what data to put into them.

I had someone ask to use DeepSeek on company data because it was free. No awareness of where that data would be processed or stored, no understanding of what the organization's acceptable use policy said, because there was no acceptable use policy. The request was well-intentioned. The risk was significant.

DeepSeek stores user data in the People's Republic of China. China's National Intelligence Law requires organizations to cooperate with state intelligence work, meaning the Chinese government can compel access to stored data. Security researchers have found DeepSeek transmitting user data to third parties without consent. Multiple governments have banned it from government use. Putting company data into a free AI tool with no enterprise data protections is not a hypothetical risk. It is a documented one.

This is what happens in the absence of a top-down AI strategy. Employees are not trying to create security incidents. They are trying to do their jobs faster. Without clear governance, they will reach for whatever tool is available.

---

## What Getting This Right Actually Requires

The organizations that successfully adopt AI tools treat the methodology as a sequence, not a checklist to skip.

Start with Envision. Get executive alignment on what AI is supposed to accomplish. Name the use cases. Define what success looks like in measurable terms. This conversation cannot happen in IT. It has to happen at the leadership level.

Then do the technical readiness work before purchasing or deploying anything. Audit who has access to what in SharePoint and OneDrive. Configure sensitivity labels. Set up DLP policies that reflect how your data actually needs to be protected. Make sure every user has MFA. These are not Copilot prerequisites in isolation. They are the baseline security posture your organization should already have. If you do not have them, AI deployment is going to surface that gap in the most visible way possible.

Purchase once the environment is ready. Train champions before you roll out broadly. Then deploy, measure, and expand.

---

## This Is a Leadership Problem

IT can configure the technical prerequisites. IT cannot create an AI strategy, define business use cases, or establish the governance culture that makes responsible AI adoption possible.

The organizations that get value from AI tools are the ones where leadership made a decision to invest in the process, not just the product. Microsoft's methodology exists because they know what happens when that process gets skipped.

Buying the license is the easy part. Doing the work that makes the license worth having is what most organizations are not willing to prioritize until something goes wrong.
